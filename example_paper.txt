Large language models are shifting catalysis from prediction-ﬁrst pipelines to workﬂow-level
coordination of hypotheses, computation, and experiments. In this Perspective, we argue that
autonomous catalysis requires closing both representation and workﬂow gaps through a veriﬁcation-
ﬁrst paradigm, in which large language model agents orchestrate heterogeneous tools, ground claims
in auditable evidence, and use uncertainty-aware validation. We further propose system-level metrics
to benchmark reliable autonomous catalysis.
Catalysis lies at the core of the modern chemical industry and sustainable
energy technologies, enabling processes such as fuel production, carbon
utilization, and environmental remediation1,2. Despite decades of progress,
catalyst discovery and optimization remain constrained by multiscale
complexity: mechanisms span multiple length and time scales, active sites
evolve under operando conditions, and performance emerges from subtle
structure–environment coupling3. These features limit purely empirical
trial-and-error and make isolated screening insufﬁcient.
Recent advances in artiﬁcial intelligence (AI), particularly machine
learning (ML), have accelerated catalysis by enabling high-throughput
property prediction and data-driven screening4–7. However, most approa-
ches remain task-oriented and prediction-ﬁrst, focusing on adsorption
energies, barriers, or material properties within narrow representations and
under ﬁxed assumptions. Consequently, these models often struggle to
compare mechanistic hypotheses, adapt to the relevant environmental
changes, or support the iterative decisions central to catalyst development.
Large language models (LLMs) introduce different opportunities. As
foundation models, they can integrate heterogeneous information, coordi-
nate tools, and generate structured hypotheses and plans8,9. Unlike con-
ventional ML models, LLMs can integrate textual knowledge with
computational outputs, experimental observations, and domain constraints
within a single decision context10,11. This capability positions LLMs as
potential coordinators of catalytic research workﬂows12.
In this Perspective, we argue that the most consequential role of LLMs
in catalysis is to enable veriﬁcation-ﬁrst catalytic reasoning, in which
language-generated hypotheses are treated as provisional claims that must
pass predeﬁned admissibility, consistency, and provenance checks before
they can update the workﬂow state or trigger downstream decisions. The
representation gap arises because mechanistic correctness is deﬁned at the
level of pathway objects (reaction networks, transition states, and kinetics),
which are rarely encoded explicitly in current pipelines. The workﬂow gap
arises because discovery is procedural and iterative, requiring decisions
about what to model or measure next under changing conditions. We
outline design requirements for physics-grounded, experiment-compatible
LLM agents emphasizing Veriﬁer checks, evidence ledgers, and uncertainty
gating and propose system-level evaluation criteria, including auditability/
provenance, robustness under distribution shift, and closed-loop sample
efﬁciency.
From catalyst prediction to catalytic reasoning
Conventional AI has improved catalytic property prediction and screening,
but these advances do not automatically translate into mechanistic under-
standing or actionable decisions. In catalysis, “correctness” is often deﬁned
at the level of pathway competition, rate-/selectivity-controlling steps, and
conditiondependence(potential,solvation,coverage),ratherthanbysingle-
point accuracy. This motivates a transition from prediction-ﬁrst pipelines
toward reasoning-ﬁrst, workﬂow-integrated systems that can formulate,
test, and revise mechanistic hypotheses through tool-grounded evidence.

These limitations can be grouped into two coupled gaps that constitute

Second, there is a workﬂow gap: catalyst discovery is procedural and

mechanism-driven discovery or autonomy because they neither encode
mechanisms as causal, testable objects nor provide a system-level procedure
for deciding what do next under changing conditions.
Catalysis as a stress test for LLM agents?
LLMs introduce a different paradigm for catalytic complexity28. Rather than
functioning only as task-speciﬁc predictors, LLMs can serve as reasoning
controllers that integrate heterogeneous information and coordinate
multistep workﬂows29. Recent work has also begun to explore agentic AI
systems for chemical discovery and catalysis, highlighting the potential of
language-drivenagentstocoordinatecomputationaltoolsandexperimental
workﬂows. These studies emphasize the importance of integrating rea-
soning, tool use, and domain knowledge within scientiﬁc workﬂows rather
than treating AI models purely as predictive components30,31. This is par-
ticularly consequential in catalysis, where knowledge spans literature,
simulations, experiments, and mechanistic hypotheses that must ultimately
be tested against energetics and kinetics.
First, LLMs can support multi-source knowledge integration by pla-
cing textual knowledge (proposed mechanisms, empirical rules) alongside
numerical outputs (energies, barriers, and kinetic parameters from density
functional theory (DFT)) and experimental observations (operando spec-
troscopy, selectivity trends, and stability signatures) with a single decision
context28. If claims are anchored to attributable retrieval and tool-generated
evidence, LLMs can help construct coherent mechanistic hypotheses across
modalities rather than treating these inputs as isolated streams10,11.
Second, LLM-based systems can enable procedural reasoning and
planning through task decomposition and tool-calling: identifying plausible
active sites, enumerating competing reaction pathways, selecting appro-
priate computational tools, and interpreting simulation outputs as evidence
arrives32,33. This mode of operation aligns with the iterative decision-making
process used by catalysis experts and directly targets the workﬂow gap of
conventional ML models.
Third, when embedded within agent-based architectures, LLMs can
shift AI from passive prediction to active workﬂow coordination. An LLM-
centered agent can decide when to run simulations, retrieve prior results,
reﬁne assumptions, or request additional data, while maintaining an explicit
task state and rationale for each action11,34. This transforms AI into an
infrastructural layer that manages hypothesis tracking, tool orchestration,
and experiment planning within a single loop (Fig. 1c).
However, catalysis also exposes fundamental constraints that LLM
agents must address. Mechanism-aware reasoning must be veriﬁcation-
ﬁrst: LLMs do not intrinsically enforce physical constraints, and therefore

LLM as the central coordinator in catalytic workﬂows
Building on the preceding discussion, LLM-centered systems can address
the representation gap by elevating mechanisms to structured, testable
objects, and address the workﬂow gap by enabling stateful, tool-mediated
“reason–act–revise” loops with delegated decision authority over action
selection. Crucially, these systems must be veriﬁcation-ﬁrst: hypotheses
proposed inlanguage are treated as provisional claims and areadmitted into
the workﬂow state only when their supporting evidence satisﬁes predeﬁned
admissibility, numerical-quality, and physical-consistency criteria enforced
by a veriﬁcation layer (Fig. 2).

which nodes encode catalytic states (site + adsorbate + environment

To enable comparison across hypotheses, catalytic mechanisms can be
represented as structured mechanism objects rather than narrative
descriptions. A minimal schema contains three components: species states,
site descriptors, and elementary steps. Species states encode the catalytic
conﬁguration as a tuple consisting of surface motif, adsorbate identity,
coverage, and environment descriptors (e.g., potential, solvation, electrolyte
conditions). Site descriptors capture the identity and evolution of the active
center, including local coordination environment, oxidation state, and
reconstruction motifs when relevant. Elementary steps connect states
through chemically meaningful transitions, annotated with energetics,
transition states, and provenance from computational or experimental
sources.
Dynamic active-site evolution can be represented through state tran-
sitions within mechanism-aware reaction graphs, where reconstruction,
oxidation-state changes, or adsorbate-induced restructuring are encoded as
state updates rather than external annotations. For example, surface
reconstruction,oxidation-statechanges,oradsorbate-inducedrestructuring
are encoded as state updates rather than external annotations, allowing
mechanisms with evolving active sites to be compared within the same
representation. In practice, competing mechanisms are evaluated by com-
bining thermodynamic feasibility, kinetic accessibility, and consistency with
experimental observations. Under partial evidence, hypotheses can be
ranked using progressively higher-ﬁdelity evaluation layers, for example,
surrogate screening, electronic-structure energetics, transition-state reﬁne-
ment, and microkinetic modeling, while pruning pathways that violate
physical constraints or contradict validated evidence. This representation
allows mechanism comparison to remain falsiﬁable and evidence-driven
even when the active site itself evolves under reaction conditions.
To keep mechanistic reasoning traceable and correctable, systems
should rely on updatable external memory and tool logs rather than para-
metric recall.Retrieval-augmented generation(RAG) combinesattributable
retrieval with generation, enabling evidence-grounded synthesis across lit-
erature and internal records with citation-level provenance38. Grounding
can be further strengthened by constraining reasoning with structured,
physics-bearing representations. Knowledge-graph–augmented LLMs can
bind pathway proposals to structured reaction objects (species, steps, con-
straints) with explicit evidence links and attribution35.
Critically, mechanism-aware reasoning must be veriﬁcation-ﬁrst:
language-generated pathway hypotheses are treated as provisional until
validated (or falsiﬁed) by physics-based tools (DFT/TS search, micro-
kinetics, stability ﬁlters) and explicit consistency checks. This design turns
mechanistic reasoning into a controlled hypothesis–test–update cycle,
enabling reliable comparison of competing pathways and condition-
dependent selectivity under a shared, auditable workﬂow state (Fig. 2).
Tool-oriented catalytic agents
The workﬂow impact becomes concrete when LLMs address the workﬂow
gap by operating in a tool-oriented, stateful “reason–act–revise” loop:
decompose tasks, invoke tools, consolidate evidence, and re-plan based on
validation outcomes and failure signals. Representative frameworks such as
ReAct and Toolformer formalize auditable tool use and specify when tool
outputs should be incorporated into subsequent reasoning32,39.
A catalysis toolchain encompasses retrieval, electronic-structure/
kinetic tools (DFT, TS search, microkinetic modeling), interface modeling
(solvation, potential, coverage), ML surrogates, structure generation, and
experimental planning (optionally automated or robotic)11. ChemCrow
illustrates that controller-plus-tools orchestration is practically realizable10.
However, reliability in catalysis requires that tool use be coupled to
veriﬁcation-ﬁrst control rather than free-form iteration. Accordingly, tool
invocation should be gated by (i) pre-checks on assumptions and inputs
(e.g., surface model validity, charge/stoichiometry, feasible coverage/
potentialranges),(ii)runtimemonitoringwithexplicitfailurehandling(e.g.,
nonconvergence, unstable structures, inconsistent energies), and (iii) post-
checks that enforce physical consistency (thermodynamic ordering, barrier
sanity, cross-tool agreement) and trigger revision when constraints are
violated.
To support auditability and reproducibility, systems should record not
only ﬁnal answers but also an evidence ledger: retrieved sources, tool calls,
parameters, versions, outputs, and decision justiﬁcations linked to each
hypothesis update. In closed-loop settings, an uncertainty gate is essential:
high-uncertainty or high-impact decisions should trigger additional com-
putation/experiments or human oversight rather than automated execu-
tion. Self-driving-lab reviews emphasize plan–execute–validate–revise
loops and traceable evidence as prerequisites for robust autonomy19.
Accordingly, outputs should be scientiﬁc deliverables rather than a
single score, including testable mechanistic hypotheses, candidate catalysts
with rationale and assumptions, experimental designs with controls, and
next-iteration queries that target dominant uncertainties. These outputs
update the task state and constraints for the next cycle, enabling sustained
integration of mechanistic reasoning with tool-grounded evidence within a
closed-loop workﬂow.
In practice, veriﬁcation-ﬁrst workﬂows do not attempt to evaluate all
hypotheses exhaustively. Instead, veriﬁcation proceeds through a multi-
ﬁdelity ladder in which inexpensive models are used to triage candidate
mechanisms before higher-cost validation is performed. For example,
descriptor-based heuristics or surrogate models may ﬁrst eliminate clearly
implausible hypotheses, followed by selective electronic-structure calcula-
tions to evaluate key intermediates. Only a small subset of surviving can-
didates may then undergo transition-state reﬁnement or microkinetic
modeling. Such staged veriﬁcation prioritizes mechanism-disambiguating
tests rather than exhaustive exploration of the hypothesis space. For
example, in an OER system competing hypotheses such as AEM or lattice-
oxygen mechanisms may ﬁrst be screened using descriptor-level energetics
before targeted transition-state calculations are performed for key steps.
To make veriﬁcation-ﬁrst control operational rather than rhetorical,
each mechanistic claim should be evaluated under a minimal veriﬁcation
policy specifying (i) the claim type, (ii) admissible evidence types, (iii)
required tool outputs, (iv) physical and numerical consistency checks, and
(v) failure responses.
At minimum, four classes of checks are required. State-validity checks
conﬁrm that the catalytic state being evaluated is physically well deﬁned,
including stoichiometry, charge/spin balance, adsorbate placement, surface
termination, and declared environment descriptors such as coverage,
potential, and solvation model. Numerical-quality checks ensure that tool
outputs are reliable for downstream reasoning, including convergence sta-
tus, force thresholds, and reproducibility across equivalent calculations.
Physical-consistency checks test whether the resulting energetics and
pathways satisfy basic constraints such as mass and charge conservation,
thermodynamic cycle consistency, and barrier sanity. Cross-evidence
checks compare simulation outputs with literature reports, operando
characterization, and kinetic trends to identify whether a claim is supported,
contradicted, or underdetermined.
Failure in these checks does not necessarily invalidate a hypothesis but
triggers a structured response: targeted reﬁnement (for example, rerunning

Emerging paradigms toward autonomous catalysis
Autonomous catalysis emerges when two advances co-develop: (i)
mechanism-aware representations that make hypotheses falsiﬁable (closing
the representation gap), and (ii) veriﬁcation-ﬁrst, tool-orchestrated work-
ﬂows that decide what to do next under uncertainty (closing the workﬂow
gap). Building on the reference architecture in Fig. 2, we highlight two
converging paradigms, LLM-guided inverse discovery and closed-loop
experimentation, that convert language-level proposals into auditable, tool-
grounded action.
LLM-guided catalyst design and inverse discovery
Conventional catalyst discovery largely follows a forward paradigm: pro-
posecandidatestructuresﬁrst,thenevaluateactivity,selectivity,andstability
through computation and experiments, and iterative reﬁne40. Despite scal-
ing relations, volcano analyses, and microkinetics, the practical logic often
remains “structure-ﬁrst, evaluate-later” (Fig. 3a)41, which can underutilize
mechanistic knowledge and make it difﬁcult to explore hypothesis diversity
under constraints.
Recent generative work reframes discovery as target-ﬁrst design: spe-
cify objectives and constraints upfront, then generate candidates for sub-
sequent evaluation. In electrocatalysis, inverse-design frameworks combine
surface generation, prediction, and optimization to map objectives to can-
didates (e.g., CO2RR-relevant surfaces)42,43. Related work on motif/envir-
onment generation further supports “inverse generation + interpretable
constraints” at the atomic scale44.
Within this landscape, LLMs can elevate inverse design from latent-
space optimization to constraint-expressible,
mechanism-actionable,
diversity-aware hypothesis generation45. Speciﬁcally, LLM agents can
translate high-level goals (activity, selectivity, stability, cost, poison toler-
ance, synthesis feasibility) into structured constraints and mechanism-
aware points, e.g., excluding pathways known to over-oxidize, enforcing
stability windows, or favoring motifs that enable a desired selectivity
bifurcation. This shifts generative design toward hypothesis diversity with
explicit rationale, rather than single-candidate optimization. Recent reviews
in catalysis and materials increasingly position LLMs, together with auto-
mation and ML, as enabling components for goal-driven discovery46.
Crucially, this paradigm does not require LLMs to “replace physics.” In
a veriﬁcation-ﬁrst workﬂow, LLM outputs are treated as provisional
hypotheses that must be ﬁltered or falsiﬁed by physics-based evaluation
(DFT energetics/barriers and microkinetic pathway competition), so that
“mechanism-conditioned generation” yields candidates that are not only
plausible but testable (Fig. 3b). In practice, this implies (i) generating mul-
tiple mechanistic hypotheses per candidate (active-site motifs, competing
pathways, selectivity control points), (ii) routing evaluation to the appro-
priate tools (including interface-aware modeling when needed), and (iii)
updating generation constraints based on validated failure modes (e.g.,
instability, unfavorable barriers, incorrect selectivity regime). This closes the
loopbetweengenerationandmechanisticveriﬁcationanddirectlyaddresses
the representation gap by treating mechanisms as ﬁrst-class objects within
the design cycle.

Characterization as a mechanistic veriﬁcation layer. In catalytic
research, mechanistic hypotheses cannot be validated by computation alone
and often require experimental characterization to conﬁrm the identity and
evolution of active sites under reaction conditions. Operando and in situ
techniques, including spectroscopy, microscopy, and electrochemical probes
play a crucial role in distinguishing between competing mechanisms and
verifying whether predicted active states exist under reaction conditions.
Within a veriﬁcation-ﬁrst workﬂow, characterization should therefore
functionasacomplementaryevidencesourcealongsidecomputationaltools.
For example, operando spectroscopic signatures, catalyst reconstruction
observed by microscopy, or isotope-labeling experiments can either support
or falsify mechanistic hypotheses proposed by simulations or literature. In
this sense, characterization data serve as high-value evidence entries in the
evidence ledger and can trigger hypothesis revision or pruning when dis-
crepancies arise. Integrating characterization into the closed-loop cycle thus
strengthens the veriﬁcation-ﬁrst paradigm by linking mechanistic reasoning,
computational validation, and experimentally observable phenomena.
Compared with earlier automation, a key differentiator of LLM-

system maintains persistent representations of goals, assumptions, con-

and provenance.
Closed-loop catalysis also hinges on information-gain-driven

Key challenges unique to catalysis-oriented LLM systems
Scope and boundary conditions. Veriﬁcation-ﬁrst workﬂows are particu-
larly valuable in catalytic systems characterized by substantial mechanistic
ambiguity, strong operando dynamics, or frequent distribution shifts
between computational models and experimental conditions. Examples
include electrocatalysis and heterogeneous catalysis under reactive envir-
onments, where surface reconstruction, coverage effects, electrolyte inter-
actions, or ﬁeld effects can signiﬁcantly alter the identity of active sites. In
such regimes, prediction-ﬁrst screening may generate candidate lists with-
out resolving the underlying reaction physics.
Conversely, in more stable catalytic systems governed by well-
established descriptors and relatively ﬁxed active sites, traditional
prediction-ﬁrst screening may remain effective. In these settings, surrogate
models or descriptor-based rankings can already provide reliable guidance
for materials selection. Veriﬁcation-ﬁrst should therefore be viewed not as a
replacementforpredictivescreening,butasacomplementaryparadigmthat
becomes particularly powerful when mechanistic uncertainty limits the
transferability of purely predictive models.
Despite growing interest in LLMs as central coordinators in catalytic
research workﬂows, their deployment in catalysis faces challenges that are
sharper than in many other scientiﬁc domains. Because catalysis is multi-
scale, mechanism-driven, and experimentally constrained, LLM systems
must be physically grounded, controllable, and experiment-compatible54–56.
Importantly, these challenges are not solved by model scale alone: they
require closing both the representation gap (mechanisms as falsiﬁable
objects) and the workﬂow gap (tool-mediated decision procedures),
implemented through veriﬁcation-ﬁrst controls (Veriﬁer, evidence ledger,
and uncertainty gate).
Mechanistic grounding and physical ﬁdelity
A primary challenge is insufﬁcient mechanistic grounding (Fig. 4a). Cata-
lytic performance depends on competing elementary steps and the TS
landscape that governs the rate and selectivity57. Without explicit pathways
and TS information, outputs remain descriptive rather than falsiﬁable. Even
in well-studied systems, microkinetic conclusions can change under com-
mon modeling assumptions, reinforcing that correlation does not imply
causation. This gap becomes sharper at electriﬁed interfaces, where opera-
tive physics depends on charge redistribution, interfacial electric ﬁelds, and
solvent- and ion-mediated stabilization. If charge, ﬁeld, and potential are
weakly represented, the system may systematically misrank barriers or
misattribute selectivity because solvation and interfacial structure can
reshape both energetics and kinetics. Comprehensive interface-focused
reviews emphasize that electrocatalytic modeling must account for
environment-dependent effects (electrostatic ﬁeld, solvation, ions, and
potential) to remain physically meaningful58.
A closely related catalysis-speciﬁc challenge is the capture of dynamic
and operando effects (Fig. 4b)25. Many workﬂows still rely on static inputs,
whereasoperandocatalysisoftendemandstime-resolvedevidence.Without
time-resolved constraints, reasoning can drift toward static-site assump-
tions, missing reconstruction, and active-site evolution under working
conditions. Operando/in situ studies emphasize that identifying the true
active species under reconstruction is central to interpreting activity and
stability.
Implication for veriﬁcation-ﬁrst design. To address this, LLM systems
must couple language-level hypotheses to physics-bearing mechanism
objects (reaction networks, step libraries, TS candidates, microkinetic
models, and interface descriptors) and require tool-grounded validation
before admitting mechanistic claims into the workﬂow state. For exam-
ple, calculations that fail numerical convergence, produce unstable
structures, or violate thermodynamic ordering should automatically
trigger recalculation, model reﬁnement, or hypothesis downgrading
rather than being converted into mechanistic conclusions.

spans diverse catalyst classes and environments, where domain-speciﬁc
overﬁtting is common and intuitive analogies can break down across
materials families (e.g., MOFs, oxides, and alloys)59,60. Moreover, failures
often arise from interfacial and solvent mismatches, in which the same
nominal chemistry behaves differently under distinct interfacial structures
or electrolyte conditions. In data-driven settings, such gaps manifest as
sensitivity to distribution shift. Large-scale out-of-distribution studies in

Claim typing and evidence admissibility. To prevent “evidence laun-
dering,” in which weak evidence is rhetorically ampliﬁed to support
mechanistic claims, the evidence ledger must enforce explicit provenance
and admissibility rules. In practice, claims should be typed according to
their epistemic status (e.g., qualitative hypothesis, mechanistic proposal,
rate-determining step assignment, or quantitative prediction), and each
claim type should specify permitted evidence sources. For instance, lit-
erature reports or empirical heuristics may support exploratory
hypotheses but are insufﬁcient to validate pathway energetics or rate
control without tool-grounded evidence such as electronic-structure
calculations, kinetic modeling, or experimental characterization.
All evidence entries should include provenance metadata (source, tool
version, input parameters, and calculation or experimental conditions) to
ensure traceability. When conﬂicting evidence arises, for example, when
literaturesuggestsapathwaythatcontradictscomputedenergeticsorkinetic
trends the claim should be downgraded to an unresolved hypothesis rather
than elevated to a conclusion. In such cases, the workﬂow should trigger
targeted veriﬁcation steps, such as additional calculations, sensitivity ana-
lysis, or experimental tests, until the discrepancy is resolved or the
hypothesis is pruned. These admissibility rules ensure that the evidence
ledger functions not merely as a record of results but as a governance layer
controlling how evidence can justify scientiﬁc claims.
Epistemic grounding and structured representations
Recent discussions in AI-driven chemistry highlight an important
explanatory trade-off: as AI systems increasingly mediate hypothesis
generation and discovery, the epistemic burden shifts toward explana-
tion, provenance, and responsibility67. In this setting, veriﬁcation-ﬁrst
orchestration can be viewed as an epistemic control mechanism that
ensures scientiﬁc claims remain grounded in traceable evidence rather
than persuasive inductive artifacts. The veriﬁer →evidence ledger →
uncertainty gate triad proposed in this work provides a concrete oper-
ationalization of this principle by enforcing admissibility rules for
mechanistic claims, recording provenance across tool outputs, and
routing high-uncertainty decisions to additional validation or human
oversight.
At the same time, the representation layer of autonomous catalysis
must integrate heterogeneous sources of knowledge, including literature,
simulations, experimental observations, and mechanistic constraints68.
Recent advances in heterogeneous knowledge graph representation
learning suggest that structured relational representations can improve
how heterogeneous signals are integrated and reasoned over. In the
context of catalytic workﬂows, such approaches are complementary to the
mechanism-object framework proposed here: graph-based learning can
help discover statistical associations or latent structural motifs, whereas
the mechanism-object layer enforces physically grounded constraints
such as reaction topology, stoichiometry, transition-state connectivity,
and microkinetic consistency. In this sense, autonomous catalysis may
beneﬁt from a hybrid paradigm in which machine learning captures
patterns across heterogeneous data while physics-based mechanism
representations deﬁne the admissible hypothesis space.
Outlook: What will truly enable autonomous catalysis
Autonomous catalysis will not be achieved by scale alone. A more plausible
trajectory is staged: LLM-assisted →LLM-coordinated →LLM-driven
agents →autonomous catalysis, where progress is evaluated by co-
development of mechanistic representations (closing the representation
gap), tool-orchestrated procedures (closing the workﬂow gap), and
veriﬁcation-ﬁrst controls (Veriﬁer, evidence ledger, and uncertainty gate)
that make decisions auditable and safe (Fig. 5a). Reviews of self-driving
laboratoriesemphasize that autonomy is operationally deﬁned by the ability
to plan, execute, and assimilate feedback within an integrated workﬂow,
rather than by model size alone19,69.

Enabling pillars for veriﬁcation-ﬁrst autonomous catalysis
Several system capabilities jointly enable this evolution (Fig. 5b). First,
mechanistic grounding depends on mechanism-aware representations that
expose explicit reaction pathways, TS, kinetics, together with environment
descriptors capturing charge, ﬁeld, solvation, and potential70,71. Without
these causal objects, systems are vulnerable to correlation-driven reasoning,
especially at electriﬁed interfaces.
Second, robust transfer across catalysts, interfaces, and conditions
requires uniﬁed representations that genuinely bridge language and struc-
ture, such as catalyst–adsorbate graphs, interface-aware encoding, and
transferable mechanistic labels72.
Third, agents must be veriﬁcation-ﬁrst by design. Rather than trusting
ﬂuent text, systems should earn credibility through domain tools (DFT, ML
surrogates, databases, microkinetics), explicit failure handling, and cross-
tool consistency checks implemented via a Veriﬁer and evidence ledger that
operationalize “no claim without evidence”11,19.
Fourth, experiment-in-the-loop learning requires adaptive scheduling
that prioritizes expected information gain and reduces uncertainty where it
matters for mechanism and decision-making73,74. This capability is most
effective when uncertainty estimates, and mechanistic sensitivity analyses
directly inform the selection of which experiments or simulations to
conduct next.
Finally, real laboratory deployment depends on trust, control, and
auditability as governance properties: veriﬁable reasoning traces, uncer-
tainty gating for high-impact decisions, standardized data schemas, and
human override and safety constraints. Veriﬁcation-centered protocols,
such as chain-of-veriﬁcation, provide concrete mechanisms to reduce
unsupported claims and improve the auditability of LLM outputs.
Conclusions
LLMs are reshaping catalytic research by extending AI beyond prediction
and screening. Rather than functioning as task-speciﬁc regressors, LLM-
centered systems can integrate heterogeneous knowledge, coordinate tool
use, and support multi-step reasoning across literature, computation, and
experimental records. Accordingly, we argue that LLMs will matter most in
catalysis as workﬂow infrastructure that earns credibility by linking
hypotheses to veriﬁcation-gated actions, rather than as a replacement for
physics-based modeling or experiments.
At the same time, autonomous catalysis remains constrained by
intrinsic catalytic complexity. Mechanistic grounding is essential: outcomes
depend on pathways and transition states, as well as environment-
dependent effects, and cannot be inferred from ﬂuent text alone. General-
ization is also nontrivial: apparent out-of-distribution success can reﬂect
interpolation, and performance often degrades under genuine distribution
shift across catalyst classes, interfaces, and operating regimes. Practical
autonomy, therefore, requires veriﬁcation-ﬁrst closed-loop operation in
which planning, execution, validation, and revision are integrated as a single
capability, with tool evidence not narrative plausibility, determining which
hypotheses survive.
Looking forward, progress will depend less on scale than on system
design and integration. We propose three design principles for reliable
autonomous catalysis:
(1) Mechanism as an object: represent hypotheses as structured, fal-
siﬁable mechanism objects (reaction graphs, TS candidates, microkinetic
models) with explicit assumptions and versioning.
(2) Veriﬁcation-ﬁrst orchestration: embed domain tools within a
controlledloop(Veriﬁer+evidenceledger+uncertaintygate)thatenforces
physical consistency, handles failures, and attaches every claim to traceable
evidence.
