\subsection{Data Resources and Benchmarks}
\label{sec:data_resources}

The empirical foundation of Financial Time-Series Generation (FTSG) relies on the quality, granularity, and structure of the underlying data. Unlike static image datasets, financial data is continuous, highly non-stationary, and prone to hidden biases. We systematically cluster these resources moving from raw modalities to curated databases, standardized benchmarks, and ultimately, the practical pitfalls of dataset construction.

\subsubsection{Raw Data Sources and Modalities}
Financial time series are not monolithic; they exhibit drastically different statistical signatures depending on the asset class and sampling frequency. The rationale for clustering these sources lies in the specific generative challenges they present. We categorize raw FTSG data into four distinct modalities:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Equities and Market Indices:} Encompassing individual stocks (e.g., AAPL, TSLA) and aggregate indices (e.g., S\&P 500, CSI 300). Sampled typically at daily or minutely frequencies, these series are the primary testbeds for unconditional synthesis and extrapolation. They present fundamental generative challenges, including pronounced \textit{volatility clustering}, \textit{fat-tailed distributions}, and \textit{regime-shifting non-stationarity}.
    \item \textbf{High-Frequency Microstructure (LOB):} Limit Order Books (LOB), tick-by-tick trades, and Level-2 quotes recorded at the millisecond or microsecond level. Generating LOB data is exceptionally complex due to its asynchronous nature and the strict spatial constraints required (e.g., bids must strictly remain below asks, and queue volumes dictate execution). This data is essential for simulating interactive, multi-agent algorithmic trading environments.
    \item \textbf{Macroeconomics and Fixed Income:} Indicators such as Treasury yield curves, the Consumer Price Index (CPI), and Gross Domestic Product (GDP). Sampled at low frequencies (monthly or quarterly), these datasets reflect systemic economic health. The primary challenge here is severe data sparsity and the need to interpolate/impute missing values across shifting long-term economic cycles.
    \item \textbf{Alternative Data and Textual Metadata:} With the rise of multi-modal foundation models, purely numerical series are increasingly augmented with alternative data. This includes macroeconomic news headlines, central bank policy texts, earnings call transcripts, and social media sentiment. This modality is crucial for training controllable generators and scenario-guided market simulators.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Curated Databases and APIs}
Historically, raw financial data is fragmented and optimized for human visualization. To facilitate the massive data ingestion required by deep generative models, researchers rely on institutional databases and developer APIs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Institutional and Academic Repositories:} For rigorous backtesting and synthesis, Wharton Research Data Services (WRDS), the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP), and Compustat provide survivorship-bias-free equity data. For macroeconomic series, FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data) remains the gold standard. In Chinese markets, CSMAR offers comprehensive, high-quality A-share trading records.
    \item \textbf{Open-Source and Developer APIs:} Platforms like Yahoo Finance and Investing.com are frequently accessed via open-source Python wrappers (\texttt{yfinance}). For programmatic, high-frequency access, APIs such as Alpha Vantage, Alpaca, and Polygon.io deliver massive historical streams suitable for training large-scale predictive models.
    \item \textbf{Cryptocurrency Exchanges:} Due to 24/7 trading and open data access, crypto markets have become highly popular for testing generative algorithms. Platforms like Binance and CoinMarketCap provide free API access to granular tick and volume data, capturing extreme volatility events ideal for stress-testing generation.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Standardized Benchmarks and Evaluation Suites}
Historically, FTSG research relied on ad-hoc, proprietary data slices, severely hindering reproducibility and fair model comparison. The recent emergence of unified benchmark platforms marks a critical maturation in the field. These benchmarks encapsulate vast datasets, pre-define chronological train/test/validation splits, and standardize evaluation metrics (e.g., masking ratios for imputation, prompt templates for forecasting). 

By providing a ``level playing field,'' these suites allow researchers to disentangle algorithmic improvements from data-mining advantages. A summary of recent and authoritative FTSG benchmarks is provided in Table \ref{tab:datasets}.



\subsubsection{Practical Issues in Dataset Construction}
Constructing financial datasets for generative models is fraught with domain-specific pitfalls. A generative architecture, no matter how mathematically advanced, will learn and amplify the structural biases present in its training data. Key issues include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Survivorship Bias:} Datasets that only include currently active assets inadvertently filter out companies that went bankrupt or were delisted. Generative models trained on such data will synthesize artificially optimistic market trajectories and fail to accurately model the probability of asset default, ruining their utility for downside risk management.
    \item \textbf{Look-Ahead Bias in Alignment:} This occurs when models are trained on information that was not publicly available at the timestamp of generation. A common pitfall in FTSG is using historically \textit{revised} macroeconomic data (e.g., revised GDP figures published months later) rather than the "point-in-time" unrevised data, causing the generator to leak future realities.
    \item \textbf{Chronological Splitting vs. Random Masking:} Unlike static images where independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random splitting is acceptable, financial time series must be strictly partitioned chronologically. Randomly splitting overlapping sequence windows leads to severe temporal data leakage. Similarly, in imputation tasks, random masking often fails to reflect the true missingness mechanisms (e.g., contiguous trading halts), leading to models that perform well on paper but fail in live environments.
    \item \textbf{Asynchronous Frequencies and Missingness:} In multivariate generation (e.g., simulating a global portfolio), assets trade on different global exchanges with distinct operating hours and holidays. Naively padding these datasets forward creates artificial zero-volatility spans, causing cross-sectional generators (like Diffusion models) to learn spurious flat-line correlations.
    \item \textbf{Market Regime Imbalance:} Historical datasets are overwhelmingly dominated by standard, low-to-medium volatility market conditions. Without specialized sampling techniques, models suffer from severe class imbalance and will systematically fail to generate realistic, high-fidelity crash scenarios (e.g., the 2020 COVID-19 flash crash) simply because these events represent a fraction of the training distribution.
\end{itemize}
