\subsection{Data Resources and Benchmarks}
\label{sec:data_resources}

The empirical foundation of Financial Time-Series Generation (FTSG) relies on the quality, granularity, and structure of the underlying data. Unlike static image datasets, financial data is continuous, highly non-stationary, and prone to hidden biases. We systematically cluster these resources moving from raw modalities to curated databases, standardized benchmarks, and ultimately, the practical pitfalls of dataset construction.

\revnote{Secondary subheading ``Raw Data Sources and Modalities'' removed; the numbered list below was converted into running prose.}
Financial time series are not monolithic; they exhibit drastically different statistical signatures depending on the asset class and sampling frequency. The rationale for clustering these sources lies in the specific generative challenges they present, and we therefore distinguish four distinct modalities.

\revadd{Equities and market indices encompass individual stocks (e.g., AAPL, TSLA) and aggregate indices (e.g., S\&P 500, CSI 300), sampled typically at daily or minutely frequencies; these series are the primary testbeds for unconditional synthesis and extrapolation, and they present fundamental generative challenges including pronounced volatility clustering, fat-tailed distributions, and regime-shifting non-stationarity. High-frequency microstructure data, comprising limit order books (LOB), tick-by-tick trades, and Level-2 quotes recorded at the millisecond or microsecond level, is exceptionally complex to generate because of its asynchronous nature and the strict spatial constraints required (e.g., bids must strictly remain below asks, and queue volumes dictate execution); it is nonetheless essential for simulating interactive, multi-agent algorithmic trading environments. Macroeconomic and fixed-income indicators, such as Treasury yield curves, the Consumer Price Index (CPI), and Gross Domestic Product (GDP), are sampled at low frequencies (monthly or quarterly) and reflect systemic economic health, with the primary challenge being severe data sparsity and the need to interpolate or impute missing values across shifting long-term economic cycles. Finally, alternative data and textual metadata are increasingly used to augment purely numerical series in the era of multi-modal foundation models: this includes macroeconomic news headlines, central bank policy texts, earnings call transcripts, and social media sentiment, and it is crucial for training controllable generators and scenario-guided market simulators.}

\revnote{Secondary subheading ``Curated Databases and APIs'' removed; the list below was converted into running prose.}
Historically, raw financial data has been fragmented and optimized for human visualization. To facilitate the massive data ingestion required by deep generative models, researchers rely on institutional databases and developer APIs.

\revadd{Institutional and academic repositories are the foundation for rigorous backtesting and synthesis: Wharton Research Data Services (WRDS), the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP), and Compustat provide survivorship-bias-free equity data, while FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data) remains the gold standard for macroeconomic series and CSMAR offers comprehensive, high-quality A-share trading records for Chinese markets. Open-source and developer APIs complement these sources: platforms such as Yahoo Finance and Investing.com are frequently accessed through open-source Python wrappers (\texttt{yfinance}), and for programmatic, high-frequency access, APIs such as Alpha Vantage, Alpaca, and Polygon.io deliver massive historical streams suitable for training large-scale predictive models. Cryptocurrency exchanges have also become highly popular for testing generative algorithms, because 24/7 trading and open data access mean that platforms such as Binance and CoinMarketCap provide free API access to granular tick and volume data, capturing the extreme volatility events that are ideal for stress-testing generation.}

\revnote{Secondary subheading ``Standardized Benchmarks and Evaluation Suites'' removed.}
Historically, FTSG research relied on ad-hoc, proprietary data slices, severely hindering reproducibility and fair model comparison. The recent emergence of unified benchmark platforms marks a critical maturation in the field. These benchmarks encapsulate vast datasets, pre-define chronological train/test/validation splits, and standardize evaluation metrics (e.g., masking ratios for imputation, prompt templates for forecasting). 

By providing a ``level playing field,'' these suites allow researchers to disentangle algorithmic improvements from data-mining advantages. A summary of recent and authoritative FTSG benchmarks is provided in Table \ref{tab:datasets}.



\revnote{Secondary subheading ``Practical Issues in Dataset Construction'' removed; the list below was converted into running prose.}
Constructing financial datasets for generative models is fraught with domain-specific pitfalls. A generative architecture, no matter how mathematically advanced, will learn and amplify the structural biases present in its training data. Five issues merit particular attention.

\revadd{Survivorship bias arises because datasets that include only currently active assets inadvertently filter out companies that went bankrupt or were delisted; generative models trained on such data will synthesize artificially optimistic market trajectories and fail to model the probability of asset default, ruining their utility for downside risk management. Look-ahead bias in alignment occurs when models are trained on information that was not publicly available at the timestamp of generation, and a common pitfall in FTSG is the use of historically revised macroeconomic data (e.g., revised GDP figures published months later) rather than point-in-time unrevised data, causing the generator to leak future realities. Chronological splitting versus random masking is a further concern: unlike static images, where independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random splitting is acceptable, financial time series must be strictly partitioned chronologically, because randomly splitting overlapping sequence windows leads to severe temporal data leakage; similarly, in imputation tasks, random masking often fails to reflect the true missingness mechanisms (e.g., contiguous trading halts), producing models that perform well on paper but fail in live environments. Asynchronous frequencies and missingness compound the problem in multivariate generation (e.g., simulating a global portfolio), since assets trade on different global exchanges with distinct operating hours and holidays; naively padding these datasets forward creates artificial zero-volatility spans, causing cross-sectional generators to learn spurious flat-line correlations. Finally, market regime imbalance is pervasive: historical datasets are overwhelmingly dominated by standard, low-to-medium volatility conditions, so without specialized sampling techniques models suffer from severe class imbalance and systematically fail to generate realistic, high-fidelity crash scenarios (e.g., the 2020 COVID-19 flash crash), simply because these events represent a small fraction of the training distribution.}
