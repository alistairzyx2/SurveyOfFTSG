\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:1}

Time-series analysis (TSA)~\citep{zhao2025timeseriesscientistgeneralpurposeaiagent,jiang2025multimodaltimeseriesanalysis} has long been a central pillar of quantitative finance, providing the mathematical foundation for modeling market dynamics, assessing risk, and pricing complex assets~\citep{box2015time}. Traditionally, TSA in finance was synonymous with forecasting: predicting the next price movement, the future volatility level, or the impending macroeconomic shift. However, as the financial industry becomes increasingly data-driven and algorithmic, the demand has evolved from mere point estimation to the comprehensive generation of plausible market scenarios. This paradigm shift has given rise to Financial Time-Series Generation (FTSG), a domain that extends beyond forecasting to include the synthesis of entirely new data, the imputation of incomplete price records, and the extrapolation of diverse financial products.

The motivation for FTSG is deeply rooted in the structural limitations of real-world financial data. Unlike text or images, financial markets are characterized by a low signal-to-noise ratio, extreme non-stationarity, and abrupt regime shifts triggered by geopolitical events or macroeconomic policy changes. Furthermore, empirical financial distributions frequently exhibit "stylized facts" such as heavy tails, volatility clustering, and leverage effects \citep{style-facts}. While historical data contains instances of these phenomena, critical events like market crashes or sudden liquidity droughts are inherently rare. Consequently, discriminative models trained solely on historical records often suffer from overfitting and fail to generalize during crises. FTSG addresses this by creating high-fidelity, privacy-preserving synthetic data that can augment training sets, simulate counterfactual stress-testing scenarios, and train robust reinforcement learning agents in simulated environments.

Across the task taxonomy of this survey, we use \textbf{FTSG} to denote the umbrella research domain (Financial Time-Series Generation), while \textbf{FTSE}, \textbf{FTSI}, and \textbf{FTSS} denote the three task-level formulations for extrapolation, imputation, and synthesis, respectively. This terminology is used consistently throughout the paper and is summarized again in the Problem Formulation and Taxonomy and Applications sections.

The rapid integration of Generative AI (GAI), as demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:timeline}, including Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) \citep{Time-GAN,wiese2020,naritomi2020data}, Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) \citep{ho2020ddpm,rasul2021timegrad}, and Large Language Models (LLMs) \citep{ansari2024chronos,Time-LLM}---into financial modeling has produced a fragmented and rapidly expanding body of literature. While several surveys have attempted to capture these advancements \citep{Survey1,Survey2,Survey3,Survey4,FinSurvey1,FinSurvey3}, they suffer from notable limitations in technical and methodological aspects.

First, the majority of existing surveys remain heavily skewed toward forecasting (extrapolation) and classification, often treating generation merely as an auxiliary data augmentation step rather than a standalone class of problems encompassing synthesis and imputation. Second, most reviews are organized strictly by model architecture (e.g., "A Survey of LLMs in Finance" or "Diffusion Models for Time Series"), which obscures the fundamental differences in task formulation, supervision signals, and conditioning contexts inherent in financial applications. Third, and most critically, existing literature lacks a systematic discussion on evaluation protocols. Assessing a synthetic financial sequence requires moving beyond generic point-wise error metrics (like MSE or MAE) to evaluate cross-sectional dependence, tail-risk preservation, and downstream utility---areas where current surveys offer little standardization or critical synthesis. Finally, there is a scarcity of resource-oriented reviews that comprehensively catalog financial benchmarks, datasets, and the practical biases (e.g., survivorship bias) that plague empirical financial research.

To substantiate these gaps concretely, Table~\ref{tab:related_surveys} positions our survey against the six prior surveys cited above along five explicit dimensions: task coverage (whether each of the three FTSG sub-tasks---extrapolation (FTSE), imputation (FTSI), and synthesis (FTSS)---is treated as a first-class topic), evaluation treatment (whether a multi-dimensional protocol is provided, beyond per-paper metric tables), dataset cataloging (whether FTSG-relevant financial benchmarks are systematically inventoried), number of references in the bibliography, and time window of coverage. The table confirms that no prior survey jointly covers all three FTSG sub-tasks with a dedicated evaluation protocol and a curated financial dataset catalog; each gap maps to one of the four motivating critiques above and motivates a corresponding contribution of our survey discussed below.

To address these critical gaps, this paper provides a systematic, evaluation-oriented survey of FTSG, focusing on the intersection of generative AI and financial econometrics over the past five years (2021--early 2026). We elevate the discourse from a mere enumeration of literature to a structured problem formulation and benchmarking perspective. Our main contributions are fourfold.

\revnote{The itemized list of contributions below was converted into running prose, so that no bolded or numbered text remains as a stand-alone list unit.}

\revadd{First, we propose a task-technique two-level taxonomy: by comprehensively reviewing 130 studies published in 2021--early 2026, we introduce a hierarchical classification system that decouples the \textit{what} (extrapolation, imputation, synthesis) from the \textit{how} (statistical, deep discriminative, GANs, diffusion, foundation models), enabling a clearer mapping of specific generative techniques to their optimal financial use cases. Second, we establish a systematic evaluation framework, that is, a comprehensive, multi-dimensional evaluation protocol for FTSG that moves beyond generic reconstruction metrics and provides a unified checklist encompassing distributional fidelity, stylized facts preservation, downstream utility, and robustness against regime shifts. Third, we contribute a comprehensive resource synthesis, compiling and categorizing a wide array of public data resources, APIs, and emerging benchmark suites (e.g., FinTSB \citep{hu2025fintsb}, TFB \citep{TFB}) that are specifically relevant to financial generation, alongside a critical discussion of practical dataset construction issues. Fourth, we identify open challenges by synthesizing the literature to expose critical bottlenecks in financial validity, and we propose actionable future directions such as financial-theory-informed generative modeling and causal representation learning.}
